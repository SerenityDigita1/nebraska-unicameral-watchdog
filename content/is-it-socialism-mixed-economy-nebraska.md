You have probably heard it at a cafe, a ballgame, or on the radio: "Socialism will break America." I am not a socialist. But I have never understood why SNAP, Medicaid, or a school lunch makes a country socialist, while a tax credit for a corporation does not. Shouldn't government work to make its people's lives better? And if we're going to argue about it, shouldn't we at least argue about the same thing?

This is the first of three pieces. This one is about the word itself, the fair case for worry, and how to talk about it. Part 2 follows the money that goes to companies. Part 3 asks whether every dollar faces the same rules.

## What socialism actually means

Merriam-Webster defines socialism as theories "advocating collective or governmental ownership and administration of the means of production and distribution of goods" ([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/socialism)). The key word is ownership. Under socialism, the state owns the farms, the factories, and the stores.

That is not what America does. The same dictionary notes that social democracies such as Sweden and Denmark "retain a capitalist system overall" ([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/socialism)). The U.S. runs a **mixed economy**: private ownership and markets, plus public spending on roads, schools, and **social insurance**, where everyone pays in and is covered against shared risks like old age, disability, or illness.

Medicare for All is the hardest case, and it deserves a straight answer. Under the leading bill, Sen. Bernie Sanders's [Medicare for All Act (S. 1655, 2023)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-118s1655is/html/BILLS-118s1655is.htm), the federal government would pay for covered care, and hospitals and doctors would get paid by signing a "participation agreement" with the government rather than being owned by it. That would be a very large expansion of government's role, and opponents have real arguments against it. But it is an argument about who pays the medical bills, not about the state seizing the means of production.

## Republicans built much of the mixed economy

- **Abraham Lincoln**, the first Republican president, signed the Homestead Act on [May 20, 1862](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/homestead-act). Daniel Freeman's claim in Gage County, filed [January 1, 1863](https://www.nps.gov/places/daniel-freeman-homestead.htm), is considered one of the first. The Morrill Act gave Nebraska [90,000 acres](https://landgrant.unl.edu/history/) toward what became the University of Nebraska.
- **Dwight Eisenhower** extended Social Security to self-employed farmers in [1954](https://www.ssa.gov/history/1950.html), saw monthly disability benefits added in [1956](https://www.ssa.gov/history/1950.html), and on [June 29, 1956](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/national-interstate-and-defense-highways-act) signed the interstate highway act: [41,000 miles](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/national-interstate-and-defense-highways-act) and [$25 billion](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/national-interstate-and-defense-highways-act) authorized.
- **Richard Nixon** signed the [1972](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/statement-signing-the-social-security-amendments-1972) amendments creating Supplemental Security Income, and the same year Congress added automatic cost-of-living increases to Social Security ([SSA](https://www.ssa.gov/history/briefhistory3.html)). (The Earned Income Tax Credit was not his. It grew out of Democratic Sen. Russell Long's "work bonus" idea ([CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44825)) and was signed by Republican Gerald Ford in [1975](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/address-the-nation-upon-signing-the-tax-reduction-act-1975).)
- **Ronald Reagan** signed the Tax Reform Act of [1986](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44825), which expanded the EITC and indexed it to inflation. He called the law "the best antipoverty bill, the best profamily measure, and the best job-creation program ever to come out of the Congress" ([Reagan Library](https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/archives/speech/remarks-signing-tax-reform-act-1986)).
- **George W. Bush** signed Medicare's drug benefit on [December 8, 2003](https://www.ssa.gov/history/briefhistory3.html), the largest Medicare expansion since [1965](https://www.ssa.gov/history/briefhistory3.html).

Democrats built pieces too: Social Security in [1935](https://www.ssa.gov/history/briefhistory3.html), federal farm payments in [1933](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-48/pdf/STATUTE-48-Pg31.pdf), Medicare in [1965](https://www.ssa.gov/history/briefhistory3.html). None of these presidents was a socialist.

## The fair case for worry

Conservatives who warn about "socialism" are often worried about things that deserve a serious hearing.

- **Debt.** CBO projects federal debt held by the public will climb to [120% of GDP by 2036](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61882).
- **Promises already made.** Social Security's retirement trust fund is projected to run dry in late [2032](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/trsum/index.html), after which revenue covers about [78%](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/trsum/index.html) of scheduled benefits. Why add programs before funding the old ones?
- **Cost of big new programs.** A [2018 Mercatus study](https://www.mercatus.org/research/working-papers/costs-national-single-payer-healthcare-system) estimated one Medicare for All bill would add about [$32.6 trillion](https://www.mercatus.org/research/working-papers/costs-national-single-payer-healthcare-system) to federal budget commitments over [2022-2031](https://www.mercatus.org/research/working-papers/costs-national-single-payer-healthcare-system).
- **Taxes and incentives.** Somebody pays, and benefits that shrink as income rises can make a raise feel like a penalty. Nebraska acted on the view that aid should reward work: Medicaid expansion work requirements began [May 1, 2026](https://dhhs.ne.gov/Documents/MLTC-WR-Requirements-FAQ.pdf).
- **Efficiency and dependency.** Government lacks price signals, and many conservatives believe families, churches, and neighbors help better than distant agencies.

These are real concerns. Notice that they are about **cost, design, and results**, not about socialism. And they apply to every public dollar, including the ones that go to companies. That's where Part 2 picks up.

## How to have the conversation

Instead of arguing, try asking:

1. What would make a program "socialist" to you: government owning it, how big it is, or who gets the money?
2. Is Social Security socialism? Is the interstate? Where is the line?
3. When a company gets a tax credit for jobs, what results should we expect? Should SNAP be held to the same standard?
4. If someone works full time and still qualifies for food assistance, who should close that gap: the employer, taxpayers, the worker, or charity?
5. Which worries you most: the debt, taxes, or work incentives? Which programs would you trim first?
6. Would cutting family aid and business incentives by the same percentage feel fair?
7. What evidence would change your mind about a program you support?

## The Nebraska angle

**SNAP.** About [150,600](https://www.fns.usda.gov/pd/supplemental-nutrition-assistance-program-snap) Nebraskans got SNAP in an average month of fiscal 2025, about [$181.65](https://www.fns.usda.gov/pd/supplemental-nutrition-assistance-program-snap) per person per month, below the national [$189.37](https://www.fns.usda.gov/pd/supplemental-nutrition-assistance-program-snap). Federal SNAP benefits to Nebraska totaled [$328.2 million](https://www.fns.usda.gov/pd/supplemental-nutrition-assistance-program-snap) that year.

**Medicaid expansion.** Voters passed Initiative 427 in [2018](https://sos.nebraska.gov/sites/default/files/doc/elections/2018/2018-general-election-official-results.pdf) with 53.55% of the vote ([356,891 to 309,533](https://sos.nebraska.gov/sites/default/files/doc/elections/2018/2018-general-election-official-results.pdf), official state canvass). As of May 2026, about [70,000](https://dhhs.ne.gov/Documents/MAC%20Meeting%20Slides%205-21-26.pdf) Nebraskans were covered through expansion, out of about [336,000](https://dhhs.ne.gov/Documents/MAC%20Meeting%20Slides%205-21-26.pdf) on Medicaid overall. The last monthly expansion report the state filed with the Legislature showed [$582.3 million](https://www.nebraskalegislature.gov/FloorDocs/109/PDF/Agencies/Health_and_Human_Services__Department_of/810_20250516-084422.pdf) spent on expansion care in state fiscal 2024-25 through April 2025, of which an estimated [$530.0 million](https://www.nebraskalegislature.gov/FloorDocs/109/PDF/Agencies/Health_and_Human_Services__Department_of/810_20250516-084422.pdf) was federal and [$52.3 million](https://www.nebraskalegislature.gov/FloorDocs/109/PDF/Agencies/Health_and_Human_Services__Department_of/810_20250516-084422.pdf) came from the state general fund.

**Farm subsidies.** Nebraska farms received [$31.9 billion](https://farm.ewg.org/regionsummary.php?fips=31000&regionname=Nebraska) in USDA subsidies from 1995 through 2025, according to the Environmental Working Group's tally of USDA records, including [$891.3 million](https://farm.ewg.org/progdetail.php?fips=31998&page=states&progcode=total&regionname=NebraskaNRCS&yr=2024) in 2024, sixth in the nation. Most Nebraskans see crop insurance and disaster aid as a sensible safety net against weather nobody controls. That's the logic of social insurance.

**Business incentives.** Through June 30, 2025, Nebraska Advantage projects used [$1.30 billion](https://revenue.nebraska.gov/sites/default/files/doc/incentives/annual_report/2025_Incentives_Annual_Report.pdf) in credits and received [$967 million](https://revenue.nebraska.gov/sites/default/files/doc/incentives/annual_report/2025_Incentives_Annual_Report.pdf) in sales and use tax refunds and exemptions, while reporting [33,203](https://revenue.nebraska.gov/sites/default/files/doc/incentives/annual_report/2025_Incentives_Annual_Report.pdf) new jobs. By our rough math (credits plus refunds, divided by reported jobs), that's about $68,000 per reported job, not counting property tax exemptions. The successor ImagiNE Act had [112](https://revenue.nebraska.gov/sites/default/files/doc/incentives/annual_report/ImagiNE_Key_Employer_FYE_6-30-25_Final_Copy.pdf) active signed agreements as of June 30, 2025, projecting [3,719](https://revenue.nebraska.gov/sites/default/files/doc/incentives/annual_report/ImagiNE_Key_Employer_FYE_6-30-25_Final_Copy.pdf) jobs. A Legislative Revenue Committee report approved by Chairman Brad von Gillern calls Nebraska Advantage "a nearly 10x return on investment" ([LR126](https://govdocs.nebraska.gov/epubs/L3770/B071-2025.pdf)). That's a real argument. The fair follow-up is the one we'd ask of SNAP: how much would have happened anyway?

Nebraska spends public money on families, farms, and firms. None of it is socialism. All of it should face the same questions: what does it cost, what does it deliver, and who does it help?
